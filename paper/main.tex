\title{SpectraLens: Weight Spectra, Loss Curvature and\\ Low-Rank Structure of Small Vision Networks}
\author{Huy Pham}
\date{November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study three small image classifiers (a VGG-style CNN, ResNet-18 and a tiny ViT) trained from
scratch on EuroSAT and CIFAR-10 through the lens of numerical linear algebra. All matrix
algorithms (power iteration, Lanczos with full reorthogonalization, randomized SVD, Hutchinson
and Hutch++ trace estimation, stochastic Lanczos quadrature, Hessian-vector products) are
implemented from scratch and verified against reference solvers. We find that (i) at
initialization every layer matches the Marchenko--Pastur prediction for random matrices, while
training halves the stable rank of convolutional layers, pushes 5--15\% of the eigenvalues out of
the noise bulk and makes the spectral tails heavy (Hill exponent $9$--$13 \to 3$--$4.6$); (ii)
growing the batch size from 32 to 512 makes the final minimum $5.4\times$ sharper (top Hessian
eigenvalue $44.5 \to 240.7$) and costs $1.2$ points of test accuracy, while SAM lowers the top
eigenvalue by $44\%$ without improving accuracy on this dataset; (iii) Tucker-2 compression keeps
the CNN at full accuracy with $45\%$ of the multiply-accumulates after a two-epoch fine-tune and
ResNet-18 within $0.33$ points at $59\%$, whereas the ViT is close to full rank and barely
compresses; and (iv) a layer's stable rank only weakly predicts how much accuracy is lost when
that layer alone is truncated (Spearman $\rho = 0.31$ on ResNet-18).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A trained network is, to a large extent, a collection of matrices. Their spectra say how much of
each layer is structure and how much is indistinguishable from noise; the spectrum of the loss
Hessian says how sharp the minimum is that the optimizer found; and the singular-value decay of a
layer bounds how well it can be replaced by a cheaper low-rank factorization. This report asks
three questions on deliberately small models, where every quantity can be computed and checked:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How do weight spectra evolve during training, and how far do they move from a random
        (Marchenko--Pastur) baseline?
  \item How do the top Hessian eigenvalues and the Hessian trace depend on the batch size, and
        what does sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) change?
  \item How much accuracy does low-rank compression cost per multiply-accumulate saved, and does
        the spectrum predict which layers are safe to compress?
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:toolkit} describes the linear algebra and the guarantees it relies on,
Section~\ref{sec:setup} the models and data, and Sections~\ref{sec:spectra}--\ref{sec:compress}
the three experiments. All numbers are reproducible with the commands in
Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\section{Numerical toolkit}
\label{sec:toolkit}

Every routine accepts either a dense matrix or a matrix-free operator $v \mapsto Av$, so the same
code runs on a $4096 \times 4096$ test matrix and on the Hessian of a network with $10^7$
parameters.

\subsection{Best low-rank approximation}

\begin{theorem}[Eckart--Young--Mirsky~\cite{eckart1936}]
Let $A = U\Sigma V^\top$ with singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots$ and let
$A_k = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^\top$ keep the top $k$ triplets. For every $B$ of rank at most $k$,
\[
\|A - B\|_2 \ge \|A - A_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1},
\qquad
\|A - B\|_F \ge \|A - A_k\|_F = \Big(\sum_{j>k} \sigma_j^2\Big)^{1/2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch (spectral norm)]
If $\operatorname{rank}(B) \le k$, its null space has dimension at least $n-k$, so it intersects
the $(k+1)$-dimensional span of $v_1,\dots,v_{k+1}$ in a unit vector $w$. Then
$\|(A-B)w\| = \|Aw\| \ge \sigma_{k+1}$, because $w$ lies in a subspace on which $A$ stretches every
vector by at least $\sigma_{k+1}$.
\end{proof}

Truncated SVD is therefore optimal per layer; the compression experiments test how far
``optimal per layer'' is from ``harmless for the network''. Our test suite checks that the rank-$r$
factorization of a random layer has spectral error equal to $\sigma_{r+1}$ to $10^{-5}$ relative
precision.

\subsection{Randomized SVD}

The randomized range finder of Halko, Martinsson and Tropp~\cite{halko2011} samples
$Y = (AA^\top)^q A \Omega$ with a Gaussian $\Omega \in \R^{n\times(k+p)}$, orthonormalizes $Y = QR$
and computes the SVD of the small matrix $Q^\top A$.

\begin{theorem}[\cite{halko2011}, Thm.~10.6 and Cor.~10.10]
With oversampling $p \ge 2$ and no power iterations,
\[
\E \|A - QQ^\top A\|_2 \le \Big(1 + \sqrt{\tfrac{k}{p-1}}\Big)\sigma_{k+1}
+ \frac{e\sqrt{k+p}}{p}\Big(\sum_{j>k}\sigma_j^2\Big)^{1/2},
\]
and $q$ power iterations replace $\sigma_j$ by $\sigma_j^{2q+1}$ inside the bound, then take the
$(2q+1)$-th root, so the excess over $\sigma_{k+1}$ decays geometrically in $q$.
\end{theorem}

The bound is checked against Hypothesis-generated matrices in the test suite. On a
$4096 \times 4096$ matrix with $\sigma_i = 1/i$ (Table~\ref{tab:linalg}), two power iterations
bring the rank-64 approximation within $2\%$ of the optimal error $\sigma_{65}$ at
$650\times$ less time than a full SVD.

\subsection{Lanczos and its convergence}

$m$ steps of Lanczos build an orthonormal Krylov basis $Q_m$ with $AQ_m = Q_m T_m + \beta_m q_{m+1}
e_m^\top$, $T_m$ tridiagonal; the eigenvalues of $T_m$ (Ritz values) approximate the extreme
eigenvalues of $A$. In floating point the basis loses orthogonality once a Ritz value
converges and spurious copies (``ghosts'') appear; we reorthogonalize every new vector against
the whole basis twice (``twice is enough''), at $O(nm^2)$ extra work. Breakdown is declared when
$\beta_j < \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\|A\|$, a tolerance chosen so that numerically rank-deficient test
matrices (null eigenvalues $\sim 10^{-16}$) still stop cleanly.

\begin{theorem}[Kaniel--Paige--Saad~\cite{saad2011}]
Let $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n$, $\phi$ the angle between the start vector and
the top eigenvector, and $\gamma = (\lambda_1-\lambda_2)/(\lambda_2-\lambda_n)$. The largest Ritz
value after $m$ steps satisfies
\[
0 \le \lambda_1 - \theta_1^{(m)} \le (\lambda_1 - \lambda_n)
\left(\frac{\tan\phi}{T_{m-1}(1+2\gamma)}\right)^2,
\]
where $T_{m-1}$ is the Chebyshev polynomial of degree $m-1$.
\end{theorem}

Because $T_{m-1}(1+2\gamma)$ grows like $e^{2(m-1)\sqrt{\gamma}}$, Lanczos needs roughly
$1/\sqrt{\gamma}$ steps where power iteration needs $1/\gamma$; on our test matrix, 60 steps give the
top 10 eigenvalues to $3.8\times10^{-6}$ in 28~ms versus 248~ms for a dense solver.

\subsection{Trace estimation and spectral density}

For a symmetric $A$ and probes $z$ with $\E[zz^\top] = I$, Hutchinson's estimator~\cite{hutchinson1989}
$\tr(A) \approx \frac1s\sum_i z_i^\top A z_i$ is unbiased with single-probe variance
$2(\|A\|_F^2 - \sum_i A_{ii}^2)$ for Rademacher probes. Hutch++~\cite{meyer2021} first computes the
trace of $A$ restricted to an approximate top eigenspace exactly and applies Hutchinson only to
the deflated remainder, needing $O(1/\varepsilon)$ instead of $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ products for
relative error $\varepsilon$ on PSD matrices.

The full eigenvalue \emph{density} $\phi(t) = \frac1n\sum_j \delta(t-\lambda_j)$ comes from
stochastic Lanczos quadrature (SLQ)~\cite{lin2016,ghorbani2019}. For a unit probe $v$, $m$
Lanczos steps give $T_m = S\,\mathrm{diag}(\theta)\,S^\top$, and
\[
v^\top f(A)\, v \;\approx\; \sum_{i=1}^m \tau_i\, f(\theta_i), \qquad \tau_i = S_{1i}^2,
\]
which is exactly the $m$-point Gauss quadrature rule for the spectral measure of $v$ and is
exact for polynomials of degree up to $2m-1$. Averaging over random probes
($\E[vv^\top] = I/n$) and smoothing each node with a Gaussian kernel gives the density plots
in Figure~\ref{fig:hessian}; $f(t)=t$ recovers the Hutchinson trace estimate.

\subsection{Hessian-vector products}

For a loss $L(\theta)$ with gradient $g$, Pearlmutter's identity~\cite{pearlmutter1994} $Hv = \nabla_\theta(g^\top v)$ costs
about two backward passes and never forms the $P \times P$ Hessian (for ResNet-18, $P \approx 1.1
\times 10^7$ and the dense Hessian would need about 500~TB in float32). Our batched operator
recomputes the double-backward graph batch by batch on every product, weighting by batch size, so
memory stays at one batch; a test checks it equals the Hessian of the concatenated batch exactly.

\section{Models and data}
\label{sec:setup}

\section{Weight spectra}
\label{sec:spectra}

Each weight is viewed as a matrix ($W \in \R^{c_{\text{out}} \times c_{\text{in}}k^2}$ for
convolutions; attention's packed projection is split into $W_Q, W_K, W_V$), and three
quantities are tracked:

\begin{definition}
The \emph{stable rank} is $\srank(W) = \|W\|_F^2/\|W\|_2^2 \in [1, \operatorname{rank} W]$; we report it
as a fraction of $\min(m,n)$. The \emph{outlier fraction} is the share of eigenvalues of
$WW^\top/n$ above the Marchenko--Pastur edge $\lambda_+ = \sigma^2(1+\sqrt{m/n})^2$ ($m \le n$),
where $\sigma^2 = \|W\|_F^2/(mn)$ is the entry variance. The \emph{tail exponent} $\alpha$ is the
Hill estimate $\hat\alpha = 1 + k\,/\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log(\lambda_i/\lambda_{k+1})$ on the largest
$10\%$ of the eigenvalues.
\end{definition}

The Marchenko--Pastur law~\cite{marchenko1967} says that for a matrix of i.i.d.\ entries with
variance $\sigma^2$ the eigenvalues of $WW^\top/n$ fill $[\sigma^2(1-\sqrt q)^2,\sigma^2(1+\sqrt
q)^2]$, $q = m/n$, as $m,n\to\infty$; anything outside that bulk is structure that noise of the same
scale cannot produce. Heavy-tailed self-regularization~\cite{martin2021} associates
$\alpha \in [2,4]$ with well-trained layers.

At initialization every network sits inside the bulk, which validates the measurement itself
(Table~\ref{tab:spectra}, Figure~\ref{fig:spectra}). Training concentrates the convolutional
weights: stable rank roughly halves, 7.6--15\% of eigenvalues leave the bulk, and the tails reach
the $\alpha \approx 3$--$4.6$ range. The small CNN acquires most of this structure in the first
epoch, ResNet-18 and the ViT gradually. On EuroSAT the ViT moves least, in line with its lower
accuracy, which foreshadows Section~\ref{sec:compress}.

\section{Loss curvature}
\label{sec:hessian}

We measure the Hessian of the mean training loss on a fixed, class-balanced set of 512
un-augmented training images, with the network in evaluation mode (so batch normalization is a
fixed affine map). Per checkpoint: the top three eigenvalues from 30 Lanczos steps, and the trace
and density from SLQ with two probes of 40 steps, about 110 Hessian-vector products in total.
The small CNN is retrained on EuroSAT with batch sizes 32, 128, 256 and 512 (everything else
fixed) and once with SAM~\cite{foret2021} ($\rho = 0.05$) at batch 128.

Larger batches end in sharper minima (Table~\ref{tab:hessian}): from batch 32 to 512 the top
eigenvalue grows $5.4\times$ and the trace $5.2\times$, monotonically, and test accuracy drops by
1.2 points, the picture of~\cite{keskar2017} reproduced at small scale. SAM does exactly what it
optimizes for, cutting $\lambda_{\max}$ by $44\%$ and the trace by $71\%$ relative to plain batch
128, below even the batch-32 run, but it does not improve accuracy here ($-0.37$ points). On a
dataset where every run is already at 97--98\%, flatness and accuracy are correlated across batch
sizes without SAM's extra flatness translating into accuracy; with one seed per run, differences
below about 0.3 points are within noise. Curvature jumps from $\approx 0.5$ at initialization to
its peak within the first two epochs and relaxes as the learning rate decays
(Figure~\ref{fig:hessian}).

The cost is dominated by the convolution double backward, for which cuDNN has no fast kernels:
a single Hessian-vector product over 2048 images took 2.4~s on the laptop GPU, so the estimate
uses 512 images (about 72~s per checkpoint, 43 minutes for all 35).

\section{Low-rank compression}
\label{sec:compress}

A linear layer $W \in \R^{m\times n}$ is replaced by its rank-$r$ truncated SVD, i.e.\ two layers
through an $r$-dimensional bottleneck, and a $k\times k$ convolution either by an SVD of its
$c_{\text{out}} \times c_{\text{in}}k^2$ matrix (a $k\times k$ convolution to $r$ channels followed by a
$1\times1$) or by a Tucker-2 decomposition~\cite{kim2016}: HOSVD on the output- and input-channel
modes gives
\[
\mathcal{W} \approx \mathcal{G} \times_1 U_{\text{out}} \times_2 U_{\text{in}},
\qquad \mathcal{G} \in \R^{r_{\text{out}}\times r_{\text{in}}\times k\times k},
\]
implemented as $1\times1$ ($c_{\text{in}}\to r_{\text{in}}$), $k\times k$ core
($r_{\text{in}}\to r_{\text{out}}$), $1\times1$ ($r_{\text{out}}\to c_{\text{out}}$). Ranks keep a
fraction (``energy'') of the squared singular values of each unfolding, and a layer is replaced only
if the factorization has fewer parameters. Costs are counted in multiply-accumulates (MACs) from
one forward pass, including attention's $QK^\top$ and $AV$ products. After compression the network
is evaluated as is and after two epochs of fine-tuning (SGD, learning rate 0.01, cosine).

Tucker-2 dominates SVD on convolutions at every budget, because it compresses both channel modes
instead of folding the spatial kernel into one side (Table~\ref{tab:compress},
Figure~\ref{fig:compress}). Two epochs of fine-tuning recover almost everything: the CNN keeps its
accuracy at 45\% of the MACs and ResNet-18 loses 0.33 points at 59\%. Fine-tuning the dense models
the same way changes them by at most $+0.1$ points, so this is recovery rather than additional
training. The ViT barely compresses: at 95\% energy no factorization is smaller than the dense
layer, consistent with its weight spectra moving least in Section~\ref{sec:spectra}.

\paragraph{Does stable rank predict sensitivity?} Each eligible layer is truncated alone to
$25\%$ of its rank and the accuracy drop (no fine-tuning) is compared with the layer's stable-rank
ratio. The Spearman correlation is $+0.70$ over the CNN's 5 layers, $+0.31$ over ResNet-18's 19
and $-0.22$ over the ViT's 12 (Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}). The sign is the expected one on the
CNNs, but with $\rho \approx 0.3$ on ResNet-18 the spectrum alone is not a rule one could choose
ranks by; where a layer sits in the network matters at least as much.

\section{Limitations}

Every configuration was trained with a single seed, so differences of a few tenths of a point
are not significant. The Hessian is estimated on 512 images in evaluation mode, which is not the
function SGD actually optimizes with batch normalization in training mode. The compression study
uses an energy threshold shared by all layers; per-layer rank selection (e.g.\ by measured
sensitivity or a FLOP budget) would likely do better than any of the uniform settings reported.
The models are small by modern standards, chosen so that every spectrum could be computed
exactly; whether the same trends hold for large pretrained networks is not tested here.

\section{Conclusion}

Training turns random-looking weight matrices into low stable-rank, heavy-tailed ones within the
first few epochs; larger batches end in sharper minima that generalize slightly worse, and SAM
flattens the minimum without, on this dataset, improving accuracy; and the low-rank structure that
training creates in convolutional layers can be cashed in as a 41--55\% reduction in compute at
little or no accuracy cost after a short fine-tune, while spectra by themselves are only a weak
guide to which layers to compress.
